# Case Study: AI + Quantum Computing Working Together

**Applying the HESI–5P Innovation Model to a future-facing partnership between human intelligence, artificial intelligence, classical computing and quantum computing.**

> Status: illustrative working case study · August 2026

![Six-stage workflow showing humans defining the problem, AI analysing information, a quantum computer tackling specialised calculations, classical systems processing results, AI interpreting the findings, and humans making the decision](../../assets/ai-quantum-hesi-5p-infographic.jpeg)

## Accessible description of the infographic

The infographic presents a six-stage cycle. Humans first define a problem, goals and constraints. AI analyses relevant information and identifies patterns. A quantum computer receives a well-defined specialised calculation. Classical systems return, integrate and securely store the results. AI analyses and compares possible options and explains the findings. Humans then evaluate ethics, safety, cost, environmental and societal effects before making the final decision. A feedback loop uses outcomes to improve later data, models and decisions.

The enabling infrastructure includes cloud or supercomputing, secure data management, orchestration, reliability, error mitigation and quantum hardware. Illustrative application areas are clean energy, healthcare, climate and environment, transport and logistics, agriculture and food security, and advanced manufacturing.

## Case-study question

How could these four complementary capabilities work together on complex problems while protecting human rights, safety, inclusion, environmental sustainability and public accountability?

## Proposed operating model

| Participant or system | Appropriate role | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **People and affected communities** | Define the need, values, constraints, acceptable trade-offs and success measures | Participation must be meaningful, not symbolic |
| **Subject-matter experts** | Validate the problem, evidence, methods, outputs and operational implications | Expertise does not remove the need for accountability or challenge |
| **AI systems** | Organise information, identify patterns, generate or compare options and support explanation | AI output is evidence to examine, not an accountable decision |
| **Quantum computing** | Attempt a clearly specified specialised calculation where a credible technical case exists | It is not a universal accelerator or a replacement for classical computing |
| **Classical computing** | Prepare workloads, control systems, process and validate outputs, and support secure operations | Conventional baselines remain essential for comparison |
| **Accountable decision-makers** | Weigh evidence, uncertainty, rights, risks, distribution and public value; decide, document and review | Responsibility cannot be delegated to a model or machine |

## Six-stage workflow

### 1. Humans define the problem

- Establish the public-interest need and desired outcome.
- Identify affected groups, especially people who may be excluded or disproportionately burdened.
- Set safety, equality, accessibility, privacy, environmental and financial constraints.
- Record credible alternatives, including a classical-only approach and taking no action.

### 2. AI analyses information

- Prepare and explore data from appropriate, lawful and documented sources.
- Identify patterns, gaps, contradictions and candidate problem formulations.
- Record model limitations, data quality, uncertainty and potential bias.
- Keep sensitive or high-risk data under proportionate human and technical controls.

### 3. Quantum computing tackles a specialised calculation

- Translate only a suitable, well-defined subproblem into a quantum or hybrid method.
- State why the proposed method may offer value over the best available classical baseline.
- Document hardware, software, noise, error mitigation, reproducibility and resource assumptions.
- Stop if the quantum component adds complexity or resource use without credible benefit.

### 4. Classical systems process the results

- Integrate quantum and non-quantum results in controlled infrastructure.
- Test validity, stability, security and reproducibility.
- Compare performance, cost and environmental effects against agreed baselines.
- Preserve an audit trail of data, code, configuration, results and human interventions.

### 5. AI interprets and compares

- Compare multiple options and surface material differences.
- Explain assumptions, uncertainty and known limitations in accessible language.
- Avoid presenting generated explanations as proof that a result is correct.
- Provide source evidence so qualified people can independently examine important claims.

### 6. Humans decide and remain accountable

- Weigh rights, ethics, safety, equality, cost, environmental effects and societal value.
- Record the decision, named owner, evidence, dissent, safeguards and conditions.
- Provide appropriate notice, challenge, appeal and redress routes.
- Monitor outcomes and revise, pause or stop the deployment when evidence changes.

## Five-P impact assessment

| Lens | Potential contribution | Risks to control | Example measures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **People** | Better health research, safer systems, useful knowledge and improved services | Bias, unsafe recommendations, surveillance, exclusion and loss of autonomy | Safety events; accessibility conformance; affected-group participation; outcome gaps between groups |
| **Planet** | Cleaner energy, climate modelling, lower waste and improved resource use | Electricity and water demand, hardware impacts, e-waste and rebound effects | Energy and water per useful outcome; life-cycle emissions; avoided emissions; material recovery |
| **Prosperity** | Scientific discovery, productivity, skills, resilient infrastructure and new economic activity | Concentrated ownership, unaffordable access, insecure work and overstated return | Cost per verified outcome; decent jobs; workforce training; SME and local participation; benefit distribution |
| **Peace** | Stronger evidence, transparent governance and safer decision support | Cybersecurity threats, dual use, unaccountable decisions and weakened public trust | Security incidents; audit coverage; recorded human decisions; complaints, appeals and remedies |
| **Partnership** | Collaboration between communities, researchers, government, business and civil society | Token participation, closed systems and unequal bargaining power | Stakeholder diversity; co-designed requirements; shared standards; independent evaluation; knowledge transfer |

## Illustrative application areas

These are hypotheses to test, not guaranteed outcomes.

- **Clean energy:** grid optimisation, materials research, storage design and selected simulation problems.
- **Healthcare:** molecular research, protein or materials modelling and carefully governed decision support.
- **Climate and environment:** modelling, risk analysis, resource planning and research into lower-carbon processes.
- **Transport and logistics:** scheduling, routing and network optimisation under real operational constraints.
- **Agriculture and food security:** selected optimisation and modelling problems involving resources, yields and waste.
- **Advanced manufacturing:** materials discovery, process optimisation and predictive analysis.

Every proposal should first ask whether a well-designed classical method can meet the need more simply, safely, affordably and sustainably.

## Governance gates

A project should not progress unless it can answer each gate with proportionate evidence.

1. **Need:** Is there a clear problem and public or user benefit?
2. **Participation:** Have affected groups helped shape the goals, constraints and measures?
3. **Baseline:** Is there a fair comparison with current practice and strong classical approaches?
4. **Technical validity:** Are the method, uncertainty and limitations documented and independently testable?
5. **Rights and safety:** Are equality, accessibility, privacy, security, safety and redress addressed?
6. **Sustainability:** Does the full life-cycle assessment support a credible net benefit?
7. **Accountability:** Is a named person or body authorised, informed and answerable for the decision?
8. **Evaluation:** Are measures, review dates, publication commitments and stop conditions agreed?

## Priority risks and safeguards

| Risk | Safeguard |
| --- | --- |
| Claims of quantum advantage without a fair benchmark | Pre-register evaluation criteria and compare with strong classical baselines |
| Poor-quality, biased or unlawfully used data | Document provenance, purpose, quality and legal basis; test effects across relevant groups |
| AI explanations that appear confident but are unsupported | Link material claims to evidence and require qualified human validation |
| Cybersecurity, integrity or dual-use threats | Apply threat modelling, access controls, monitoring, incident response and appropriate disclosure controls |
| Environmental costs exceeding the claimed benefit | Measure the full system boundary and apply an energy, water, carbon, materials and e-waste gate |
| Benefits concentrated among a small number of organisations or communities | Include fair-access, skills, procurement and benefit-sharing measures |
| Accountability blurred across vendors and systems | Assign named owners, define responsibilities contractually and maintain a decision and audit record |
| High-impact use deployed too quickly | Use staged pilots, independent evaluation, human review and explicit pause or stop conditions |

## Measurement framework

Before a pilot begins, record a baseline and a small set of decision-relevant indicators.

| Category | Candidate indicators |
| --- | --- |
| **Technical** | Accuracy or solution quality; reproducibility; uncertainty; reliability; end-to-end time; performance against classical baseline |
| **People** | Safety incidents; accessibility barriers; participation; satisfaction; outcome gaps; complaints and successful remedies |
| **Planet** | Energy, water and carbon per verified outcome; hardware life; e-waste; biodiversity effects; net environmental benefit |
| **Prosperity** | Total cost; cost per outcome; decent jobs; skills gained; local and SME participation; affordability |
| **Peace** | Audit completion; security events; privacy incidents; decision records; challenges, appeals and response times |
| **Partnership** | Participating stakeholder groups; co-designed changes; research or standards collaboration; knowledge shared |

Metrics should be disaggregated where lawful, safe and necessary to detect unequal effects. A project should report negative and inconclusive findings as well as successes.

## Current limitations

- Quantum computers are specialised and developing systems, not general-purpose replacements for classical computers.
- Feasibility and any advantage depend on the exact problem, algorithm, hardware, errors, integration costs and comparison method.
- A faster calculation does not automatically create a better, fairer or more sustainable real-world outcome.
- AI-generated interpretation can be incomplete or wrong and requires evidence-linked human validation.
- Sensitive and high-impact applications require domain-specific law, standards, assurance and professional oversight beyond this model.

## Recommended next steps

1. Validate the workflow with quantum, AI, sustainability, equality, accessibility, security and domain experts.
2. Select one bounded, non-critical pilot with a strong classical baseline.
3. Co-design goals, constraints, indicators and stop conditions with affected groups.
4. Measure the complete technical, social, environmental and economic system boundary.
5. Commission independent evaluation and publish positive, negative and inconclusive results.
6. Use the feedback to revise both the application and the HESI–5P assessment method.
